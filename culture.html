Practical Life

Culture & Community

Language practice, everyday culture shocks, friendships, and honest talk about the harder parts of living abroad.

Improving Your French

Arriving without full fluency is completely normal, and immersion genuinely helps faster than you'd expect. A few things that help along the way:

  • Keeping a diary in French
  • Using Duolingo or similar apps
  • Listening to French podcasts during your commute
  • Reading French books, and watching French TV shows and films
  • Practicing with real people whenever you can — colleagues, friends, and language exchange or polyglot clubs are all great for this; nothing replaces actual conversation

If you're considering a Master's program later, you may need a placement exam like the DALF, DELF, or TCF — prep books are widely available in bookstores and online, and if your school has access to GlobalExams, it's a useful practice resource. It's also worth picking up some informal/slang vocabulary, mainly so you can recognize and manage it appropriately if it comes up in the classroom.

Package Delivery

One common culture shock: French mail carriers generally won't leave packages outside your door. You need to be home to receive anything that doesn't fit in your mailbox — plan around this, or ask a neighbour to accept it for you, or use a pickup locker (often found in train stations). International packages from family or friends abroad may require a trip to the post office, and you may need to pay a customs fee to collect them.

Friendships & Social Life

Moving to a new country can feel isolating at first, and that's completely normal. Online groups for assistants (like the ones where guides like this one circulate) are a good way to meet people in the same situation. That said, try to branch out beyond just other assistants — getting to know colleagues and locals is a big part of what makes the experience worthwhile. Saying yes to a coffee or dinner invite from a colleague, or looking for local (rather than expat-focused) social spots, can go a long way toward building a life here rather than just visiting it.

Apps like BumbleBFF can help with platonic friendships specifically, separate from dating apps.

Dating & Relationships

Dating apps commonly used in France include Tinder, Hinge, and Bumble, alongside a French-specific app called Fruitz. Being clear about what you're looking for in your profile is generally appreciated. Cultural norms around dating and relationships (for example, around exclusivity) can vary a lot depending on where you and a partner are each from — it's worth taking time to understand those differences rather than assuming shared expectations.

Sexual Health

Condoms are available from street vending machines (often near pharmacies), in metro stations, and at pharmacies. For STD testing, a CeGIDD centre provides free testing, including bloodwork, for everyone — regardless of immigration or Sécu status. Appointments can be booked online, though they sometimes fill up in advance, so it's also worth trying a walk-in. Find your nearest CeGIDD: sida-info-service.org

France provides accessible reproductive healthcare, including contraception and abortion care, through the standard healthcare system, regardless of nationality or residency status. If you need this information: angloinfo.com — abortion access in France

Homesickness & the Harder Days

It's okay — genuinely normal — to have hard weeks during this experience, alongside the good ones. Moving away from your support system, and living somewhere you don't speak the language perfectly, is tiring. That doesn't make you any less grateful for the opportunity, and it doesn't take away from the good parts. It just makes you human. Give yourself real time to adjust before deciding the program isn't for you — but if you've genuinely given it a shot and are still struggling, that's a valid outcome too, and support exists either way.

If you're struggling, please reach outSi vous traversez une période difficile, n'hésitez pas à demander de l'aide — to a friend, a professional, or one of these resources:
  • 3114 — France's national, free, 24/7 suicide prevention helpline
  • SOS Help — free, confidential support by phone, in English, for the international community in France
  • 112 — EU-wide emergency number, for any urgent situation
  • A fuller directory of French helplines: therapyroute.com

It's also worth hearing the other side: plenty of assistants describe their year as close to perfect, with colleagues who went out of their way to include them, host family dinners, help with settling in, and lasting friendships that outlasted the contract itself. Experiences genuinely range widely — go in with an open, positive mindset, but without assuming everything will be effortless either way.

It's okay to bring an advocate. If you're dealing with a difficult situation — a conflict with a principal or colleague, for example — it's completely reasonable to ask a colleague, another Prof Ref, or a mentor to accompany you and help advocate, especially if language is a barrier. Most people genuinely want to help, but if a situation feels off, trust that instinct and get a second opinion from someone you trust rather than navigating it alone.

Responding to Discrimination at School

Occasionally, a colleague or student may say something racist, homophobic, sexist, or otherwise discriminatory. This is genuinely difficult to navigate as a foreign assistant — you're often without your usual support network, managing language and cultural barriers at the same time, and explaining why a comment is hurtful is harder with children than adults. This section focuses specifically on the school context.

Your feelings about this are valid, whatever they are — confusion, anger, sadness, or delayed processing weeks later are all normal reactions. Standard "just ignore it" teaching advice doesn't really apply to discriminatory remarks the way it might to a student just being rude. Talk to someone you trust — a colleague, friend, fellow assistant, or a professional. You can't single-handedly fix a much bigger societal issue, and that's okay too — but speaking up, even briefly, has real value both for you and for the students involved, including ones who may have experienced the same thing themselves.

Responding in the Moment

  1. Report what happened to a teacher or another adult straight away, and ask them to speak with the student(s) involved
  2. Where appropriate, a short 1:1 conversation with the student can help: ask them to clarify what they said ("Pardon, qu'est-ce que tu as dit ?"), explain that it was hurtful ("Ça me gêne parce que..."), and calmly ask for understanding and an apology ("Est-ce que tu comprends ? Est-ce que tu peux me dire 'désolé' ?")

Responding Afterwards

  • Report the incident to the student's teacher, and ask them to address it with the class if appropriate
  • Write down the details (date, place, names) in case it becomes a pattern
  • Speak to your school director or pédagogique advisor for support
  • Where it fits, work with colleagues to address the underlying issue through a lesson — for example, a lesson exploring your own cultural background alongside a broader discussion on not assuming someone's heritage from their appearance
  • Feedback can also be sent to FEI directly through the anonymous end-of-year survey, or via your program ambassador

Further Resources

  • France Culture's podcast L'école face aux racismes, on confronting racism in schools
  • The French Ministry of Education's official pages on Agir contre le racisme, l'antisémitisme et les discriminations liées à l'origine, searchable on eduscol.education.fr
  • Local associations running anti-racism workshops for students and educators exist in many cities — worth asking your school or rectorat about what's available locally

Practical Everyday Tips

Personal Safety

Like any city or region, some neighbourhoods are safer to walk through alone at night than others. It's worth asking your Prof Référent or local colleagues which areas locals tend to avoid, especially if you'll be walking home alone regularly. Pepper spray is legal to buy in France and can be purchased at an "armurerie" (hunting/gun store) for around €18, if you'd like to carry some.

Choosing a Region

If you say you're open to a small town, be prepared for what that can actually mean — some French villages have only a few hundred residents. That's not necessarily a problem (it can suit introverts well), but public transport in small towns is often limited (sometimes just a few buses a day, none on weekends), so factor weekend trips out of town into your budget if you think you'll want a change of scenery regularly — not just occasional holidays, but ordinary free weekends too.

Everyday Money-Savers

  • Lidl is a popular budget-friendly supermarket chain across France
  • Most museums, monuments, and many other things are free or discounted for under-26s — a passport or ID card is worth carrying for identity checks when claiming these discounts

VPNs

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) is a paid service that routes your internet connection through a server in another country — useful for accessing content only available in certain countries (like some streaming platforms), or for extra privacy on public wifi. Several reputable providers exist at a range of price points; it's worth comparing a couple before choosing one, since pricing and features change often. One practical note some assistants have run into: if your VPN provider's website is set to French and isn't working as expected, try switching the site language to English before troubleshooting further.

A Few More Small Things

  • Scan or photograph your important documents with your phone as a backup, in case a physical copy is ever lost
  • Keep a physical binder or folder for paperwork — almost everything administrative in France is still done on paper, and you'll likely need to reference old documents later
  • If you're vegetarian or vegan, it's worth paying attention to your iron intake, as some assistants report feeling noticeably low-energy without adjusting for this — this is general awareness, not medical advice, so check with a doctor if you're concerned
  • Many colleges use "ProNote" to communicate with staff and students — ask for your access code when you arrive so you're not missing communications
