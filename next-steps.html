Looking Ahead

Renewal, Leaving & What Comes Next

This program has good times and hard times, like any job. Whatever you decide is right for you, here's what each path actually involves.

Leaving the Program Early

Quitting is a serious decision, and only you know what's genuinely best for your situation — there's no judgment in choosing to leave if that's the right call for you.

  • Start by contacting your Professeur Référent, who can guide you on next steps
  • You'll likely also need to contact FEI (assistant@france-education-international.fr) and your academy/rectorat — how much of this falls to you versus your secretary varies by situation
  • Write a resignation letter stating your exact last working day. Where possible, give notice so your academy has a chance to find a replacement
  • You'll also need to formally quit through the button on your ADELE account, in addition to the steps above
Your visa is directly tied to your job. Quitting means forfeiting your right to stay in France under that visa — trying to remain in the country afterward without authorization can lead to real immigration consequences and isn't recommended.

Second Jobs

Assistants on a Travailleur Temporaire visa are not permitted to hold any other job alongside the assistantship — including informal or "under the table" work. This is a hard legal limit tied to the visa itself, not just a guideline. The main exceptions are EU citizens, or those separately holding a student visa.

Based on France's Code du travail (Article L1221-1), Code de la sécurité sociale (Article L243-1-2), CESEDA (Articles L5221-5 and L5221-8), and the Circulaire du 18-8-2023 specific to language assistants, which states non-EU assistants cannot hold another activity, while EU-national assistants may request permission for additional work through their hierarchy.

Renewing Your Contract

The renewal process changes somewhat each year, so it's worth checking in with current assistants (via community social media groups) closer to the time for the latest specifics. A couple of general points:

  • Make sure you've listed at least a B2 level in French on your renewal application
  • Renewing typically involves a new motivation letter explaining your first year's experience, along with an evaluation from your school, submitted by your school to FEI ahead of a March-ish deadline (confirm current timing each year)
  • If you're staying in the same region, buying a round-trip ticket for your break between contracts (if you plan to return) can work out cheaper than two one-way tickets

Renewal contacts: renouvellement@france-education-international.fr, 01 45 07 60 00. Renewal tutorials and current links are published each year on the FEI website — search there directly for the current year's version rather than relying on an old link.

End of Program

If you're American, British, or from another nationality without EU/Schengen residency rights, it's widely understood that to stay in Europe afterward on a 90-day tourist basis, you need to leave the Schengen Zone (not the same as the EU — the UK and Ireland are common options) and re-enter to start that 90-day window fresh. Requirements vary by nationality, so confirm with your embassy or consulate. See our Life in France page for the current status of ETIAS, which will eventually apply to this process once it launches.

Overstaying a visa is not recommended under any circumstances — it can result in fines, being barred from the EU, and can make you ineligible for future naturalization even in a different EU country.

Your school can sign an official "attestation de services" confirming you completed your contract — mostly useful for your own records: form here

End-of-Program Checklist

  • Sign the final completion document with your school
  • Give your bank advance notice of closing your account — but don't close it before the end of summer, or you may not receive your prime (see Money & Taxes)
  • Cancel subscriptions (phone plan, internet, etc.)
  • Update your address with CAF (see Life in France)
  • Send your landlord/agency proper written notice (préavis) if ending a lease — see the Housing page for the exact notice periods and a template tool

Life After Your Assistantship

Master's Programs

Rules here are nationality-specific and change often — France's Monmaster system has shifted policy several times in recent years. Confirm everything directly with Campus France or your target university before relying on this.

Broadly: if you're already living in France, you can generally apply through Mon Master (free, deadline typically around March–April) — but you'll likely still need to go through Campus France as well, since a student visa appointment requires the documents Campus France provides. Applying through both is commonly recommended. If you're not yet living in France, Campus France (also called Études en France) is the main route, with a fee of roughly €200–250, and a deadline typically around December 31/January 1 the year before you'd start — so preparation needs to start early.

A typical Mon Master dossier includes:

  • Personal information, contact details, and your CV (in French and English)
  • Your secondary school diploma details (a translated equivalence usually isn't needed if you already have a Bachelor's degree)
  • All post-secondary certificates and degrees
  • Your Bachelor's transcript, plus an official French translation
  • Any internship, professional experience (including your assistantship), sports/arts, or scholarship information that applies to you

Most programs require proof of your French level — commonly B2, though many ask for C1 — via the TCF or DELF/DALF exam. You'll also typically need an ENIC-NARIC equivalence certificate for your Bachelor's degree specifically for the Master's application (separate from any used earlier for other purposes). This costs over €100 and can take a few months to arrive — current average processing time runs 3–5 months, so apply as early as possible. A certified translation of your transcript, a motivation letter in French, and letters of recommendation are usually required too.

Useful starting points: the Campus France programme catalogue at campusfrance.org, and scholarship information at service-public.gouv.fr. If you plan to stay in France after your studies to work, look into the RCE (recherche d'emploi) visa: service-public.gouv.fr

Lecteur/Lectrice & Maître de Langue

A step beyond the assistantship: a Lecteur/Lectrice position is similar in spirit but at university level, usually around 12 hours/week for a take-home salary in the range of €1,300–1,400/month. Unlike an assistant, you'll typically teach classes on your own rather than alongside a teacher, and exact requirements vary by university. Universities generally recruit roughly every two years, since contracts are renewable for a maximum of two years.

Maître de Langue (MdL) is the next level up from Lectrice, and always legally requires a Master's degree. Job postings for both roles circulate through community Facebook groups and occasional community-maintained spreadsheets — search "Lecteurs et Lectrices de Langue en France" and "Saesfrance.org" for current postings, since these change constantly and specific links go stale quickly. Relevant legal text: legifrance.gouv.fr

Becoming a Teacher in France (CAPES / CAFEP)

To teach English (or another subject) as a qualified French teacher, you'd typically take the CAPES exam, or its private-school equivalent, the CAFEP. As a non-EU citizen, you're only eligible for CAFEP (teaching in "privé sous contrat" schools) — EU citizens can sit either. The exam content and sections are identical between the two; CAFEP candidates just compete against a smaller pool, and passing thresholds can run higher as a result.

Exam themes, texts, and specific requirements change every session — anything describing a specific year's exam content will be out of date by the time you take it. Treat the overview below as a description of the exam's shape, not its current content.

General shape of the exam:

  • Two tracks exist: "bac+5" (requiring a Master's degree) and "bac+3" (Bachelor's level, generally considered less demanding, though may require a concurrent Master's afterward depending on your teaching background)
  • Written exams ("épreuves écrites") typically include a disciplinary essay (written in English, comparing provided documents around a set theme), a translation section (thème and version, translating between French and English), and for bac+5, an applied/pedagogical section written in French covering linguistics, phonology, and lesson planning
  • Results determine if you're invited to oral exams ("admissibilité"), typically a couple of months later, usually held in June in a city determined by your académie (transport/accommodation isn't reimbursed)

Common prerequisites: a strong French level (C1 is generally recommended, and the register of French expected is formal/"soutenu"), an ENIC-NARIC equivalence for your degree, certified translations of your diplomas and transcripts, and a background check (casier judiciaire) from your home country — all of which can take weeks to months, so start early. You'll register for the exam through the official Cyclades platform.

Preparation resources: the official "rapports du jury" (jury reports), published after each exam session on devenirenseignant.gouv.fr, are considered essential reading — they explain exactly what's expected and where past candidates lost points. Past exam papers are available through saesfrance.org. Community Facebook groups searchable as "CAPES Anglais" are also a good source of shared study material and support. If you're an assistant while preparing, your colleagues can be a genuine help — don't hesitate to ask for practice partners or borrowed textbooks.

This is a demanding, competitive exam ("concours") that typically requires months of dedicated preparation — the summary above only outlines its shape, not a study plan. Your académie and the official resources above are the right place for anything specific to your situation.

Staying On As a Local Recruit

If you already have the right to work in France independently (not on the assistant visa), you may be able to apply as a "local recruit" — these positions fill posts left vacant by assistants who drop out partway through the year, and do not sponsor a visa themselves. If interested, reach out to your académie's language assistant coordinator directly (search "[your académie] contact assistants de langues" to find the current contact, since staff and emails change).

Other Routes

  • Changing jobs/visas while on your assistant contract, if you find other employment: interieur.gouv.fr

PACS to a French Partner

In France, couples have three legal relationship options: marriage, civil partnership (PACS — Pacte Civil de Solidarité), or informal cohabitation (concubinage), each with different benefits. A PACS is a common route for a foreign partner in a relationship with a French national. A brief overview of the process:

  1. Start at the PACS section of service-public.fr, which asks a short series of questions (where you'll register, nationality, prior marriages, any legal protection measures) and gives you tailored instructions. You can register at a mairie, with a notary, or at an embassy/consulate if registering from abroad
  2. Both partners separately complete a déclaration conjointe de PACS (which includes an attestation confirming your shared residence) and a convention de PACS (either a simple or detailed version, depending on your situation), plus photocopies of photo ID
  3. Non-French partners not born in France also need an attestation de non-PACS (less than 3 months old), along with a certificat de coutume and certificat de célibat (or your country's equivalent official attestations)
Birth certificates: French nationals generally don't need to provide one, since the mairie can access civil status records directly. Everyone else needs a recent birth certificate (under 6 months old, ideally under 3 — French civil documents are considered "living" and reflect ongoing status changes). If it's not in French, it needs official translation, and depending on your country, may also need an apostille or légalisation. US citizens specifically need an apostilled birth certificate.

Translations must come from an officially certified translator (traducteur assermenté/agréé) — the official list, searchable by city: courdecassation.fr

Once both partners have completed and signed their documents, you submit them together at the relevant mairie office (some offer appointments for drop-off, others don't) — bring physical originals alongside any photocopies. After review and approval (anywhere from hours to days), you'll receive a link to book your official PACS appointment, which both partners must attend in person. The registering civil servant verifies everything once more and registers the partnership on the spot — they don't keep copies, so safeguard your own documentation afterward. If you ever lose it, your local mairie can issue a récépissé d'enregistrement (bring ID).

Requirements vary by nationality and individual circumstances — use the service-public.fr formula linked above to get instructions specific to your exact situation rather than relying only on this summary.

French Citizenship (Naturalization)

Reliable, complete information on this path is still being gathered for this guide — naturalization is a long, document-heavy process, and getting details wrong could genuinely cost you time. Treat this as a pointer to start your own research, not a full guide yet.
  • A civic knowledge exam ("examen civique") became a requirement following a January 2024 law — official info: legifrance.gouv.fr
  • General naturalization pathway info: service-public.fr
  • A couple of things worth knowing early: order extra copies of your birth certificate (and your parents' birth/marriage certificates) since translators and the prefecture may need to keep copies, and many documents "expire" after a certain period, so build a timeline rather than gathering everything at once
