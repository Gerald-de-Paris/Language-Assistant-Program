In the Classroom

Classroom Toolkit

Practical ideas for teaching English to French pupils from nursery to sixth form, written for Ugandan assistants. Use what fits, adapt the rest, and bring your own Uganda into every lesson. Your teachers know their pupils best, so always agree your plans with them first.

Where this comes from. These notes are a general collection of classroom ideas written in our own words, adapted from what assistants in different countries have shared. Games like bingo, charades and hangman are traditional and free for anyone to use. The examples here are set in Uganda.

Your Role in One Page

  • You are the assistant, not the class teacher. You support the teacher with spoken English, vocabulary, simple sentences and everyday expressions.
  • Your first job is to make pupils curious about English and about the places English connects them to, including Uganda.
  • You are an adult in the room, not a classmate. Stay warm and approachable, but be clear about limits so lessons stay respectful.
  • You are never alone in this. If something worries you, speak to your class teacher or your professeur référent.

Working With Your Teachers

  • Ask in your first week what the class is learning this term and what the teacher hopes you will cover.
  • Find out what pupils already know, and whether it is their first contact with English.
  • Set a small goal for each class group each week, and tell the teacher in advance if you want to lead a specific activity.
  • Ask for the teaching materials the school already has, especially flashcards for the current vocabulary.
  • Teachers work differently. Some give you free rein, others plan every minute and hand you a script. Ask early, so you are neither lost nor caught unprepared.
  • Ask for honest feedback after your first few sessions, then adjust.
  • Let the teacher help you with discipline. It is normal and wise.

Know the Age Groups

Maternelle (3–6)

Petite, moyenne and grande section. Short attention spans and lots of energy. Songs, movement, objects and picture cards work best.

CP & CE1 (6–8)

Mostly spoken work, little writing, often about 30-minute sessions. Routines, songs, flashcard games and simple role play.

CE2 to CM2 (8–11)

More writing and longer sessions, often about an hour. Recap old vocabulary, add new words, practise questions and answers, then short descriptions.

Collège (11–15)

Pupils are building grammar and confidence. Short speaking tasks, pairs, games with a language goal, and plenty of encouragement.

Lycée (15–18)

Conversation, opinions and debate. Topics from their lives such as phones, social media, travel and careers work well.

Session lengths differ between schools. Treat the times above as a starting guess and confirm yours with the teacher.

Maternelle: Keep It Short, Physical and Fun

  • Songs and action rhymes. Body-part songs, weather songs and seasonal songs. Do the actions with them.
  • Book reading. Read a picture book, point to objects, and invite the group to repeat the words.
  • Flashcard games. Happy families, "point to…", and "what is missing?".
  • A daily routine. Greeting, counting, the alphabet, and how the children feel today.
  • Object hunts. Ask them to find something red, or something to write with, in the classroom.
  • Plan for improvising. If a plan flops after three minutes, switch to a song and carry on.

Avoid: activities that are too hard, too many activities in one session, forcing a child to speak, depending entirely on technology, and being alone with one pupil in a classroom or corridor.

Primaire: Games That Teach Vocabulary and Sentences

Most of these need only a board, paper and pens. Always give an example first, set a time limit, and let pupils check meanings in their books or on a word list.

Theme bingo

Pick a theme (colours, animals, fruit). Pupils write 16 theme words in a 4×4 grid, in any order. Call words at random. First straight line shouts "Bingo!". Play again with a new theme. For a group version, let teams fill one big paper together.

Word search

Choose 8–12 words on one theme and hide them in a letter grid, across, down and diagonally, then fill the gaps with random letters. Teams race to find the most words.

Letter-pool words

Each pupil in a team gives the first and last letters of their own name. The team writes as many English words as it can from that pool before time runs out, then reads out and uses some in sentences.

Dictionary race

Give each pupil a French–English dictionary. Say "Look for the word…" and the first to find it and call out the French meaning scores a point.

Draw and guess

Two teams, a board split in two. One pupil from each team draws a word, with no letters or numbers allowed. Teammates shout the guess in English.

Charades

Write short sentences on slips of paper (He goes swimming every week). One pupil acts, the team guesses the full sentence within the time limit.

Running dictation

Stick short texts around the room. One partner runs to read, memorises a chunk, runs back and dictates it. The other writes. The first accurate pair wins.

Directions game

Teach go straight, turn left, turn right, stop. Put pictures of places on the board (school, market, health centre, church, bus stop). A blindfolded pupil draws a line to the place while teammates give directions.

Paper-bag skit

Give each group a bag with several everyday objects. They invent a short scene using every object and perform it in English.

Scattergories

Pupils suggest categories (river, food, animal, country). Pick a letter, shout GO, and groups write one answer per category starting with that letter. Unique answers score.

Simon says

Great for body parts and commands. "Simon says touch your nose." No "Simon says", no action.

Telephone whisper

Whisper a sentence along a line. The last pupil says what they heard. Good for listening and pronunciation.

Kim's game

Show nine pictures or objects for 30 seconds. Cover or remove them and ask teams to recall as many as possible in English.

Baggage claim

Pupils write facts about themselves on a "bag" card, cards are shuffled, and each pupil walks round asking questions to find the owner of the card they picked up.

Hangman and word wheel

Classic quick fillers. Make a spinning wheel online with categories or questions for turns and speaking prompts.

Story time that builds vocabulary

  1. Tell a short story, or invite pupils to share one in French if you need to unlock them.
  2. Ask pupils to catch one new or hard word each and write it on the board.
  3. Break the word into sounds, say it slowly and have the class repeat.
  4. Give the meaning (in French if needed) and ask pupils to build a new sentence with each word.
  5. Finish with a few comprehension questions and one question about the lesson of the story.

Choose short folk tales, ideally from Uganda, with a clear message about honesty, kindness or patience. Tell them in your own words, pause often to check understanding, and keep the vocabulary list to about five words.

Collège and Lycée: Speaking Tasks That Work

  • First-day dialogue. In pairs, pupils imagine meeting a new classmate and write a short dialogue to become friends.
  • Two truths and a lie, "presentation" version. Pupils introduce a partner and slip in false details. The class stands up whenever they hear a false one.
  • Describe an imaginary person. Put lists of appearance, clothes, colours and personality words on the board. Pairs invent a person and describe them.
  • Lost at the airport. One pupil describes a person to a shop assistant to help find them. Good for physical description and polite requests.
  • Hot seat. One pupil answers questions from the class in character, as themselves, or as a famous person.
  • Picture description. Show a photo and ask what is happening, what happened before, and what happens next.
  • Short video, then summary. Show a clip, then have groups summarise it. Test every video before the lesson.
  • Story continuation. Each pupil adds one sentence to a story.
  • Pick and act (verbs and tenses). Pupils pick a slip such as "jump and hurt your leg" and act it out. The partner names the verbs and says a sentence in the present, past and continuous forms. Three points for every correct sentence.
  • Have you ever…? Everyone writes yes or no, then one pupil asks and others answer "Yes, I have" or "No, I haven't". It practises the present perfect and helps the class get to know each other.
  • Human numbers. Pupils hold large digit cards. Call out a number such as 901 and the right pupils must line up in order within ten seconds.
  • Mini projects. Readers prepare a short news bulletin, artists make a small magazine about their country, musicians write a few lines of lyrics to a simple beat.
  • Quiz board. Draw a grid with categories (About me, General knowledge, Uganda, Class topics, Pronunciation) and point values. Include tongue twisters, numbers to read aloud, or pairs like ship and sheep.

Discussion topics older pupils enjoy

  • Smartphones and screen time: how much is too much?
  • Cyberbullying: what it is, how it feels, what helps.
  • Online fraud and fake news. A good Ugandan angle is mobile money scams: how they work and how people protect themselves.
  • Careers: ask pupils to name jobs they want, the tools they would use and key words connected with them.
  • How life is similar and different in Uganda and France: school day, food, transport, music, family.

Prepare a few guiding questions in advance, give pupils thinking time before speaking, and treat sensitive topics with care. Pupils may have experienced what you are discussing.

Bring Uganda Into the Classroom

French pupils are usually delighted when the assistant shares real life from home. Choose simple, true and positive things, show pictures, and invite questions. Say what you know, and say "I don't know" when you don't.

Where and what

Uganda is in East Africa, on the equator, with Kampala as its capital. English and Swahili are official languages, and many Ugandans speak a local language at home too.

Nature

Lake Victoria, the source of the Nile near Jinja, the Rwenzori mountains and the mountain gorillas of the south-west make a great picture set.

Food

Matooke, groundnut sauce, posho, chapati, mandazi and the street favourite called rolex (a chapati rolled around an egg omelette). Bring a recipe card or a picture quiz.

Greetings

Teach a few Luganda words: Oli otya? (how are you?), Bulungi (fine), Webale (thank you). Ask pupils to greet each other.

Names

In Buganda, twins have special names such as Wasswa and Babirye (first-born twins) and Kato and Nakato (second-born twins). Their parents are called Ssalongo and Nnalongo. Ask pupils what their own names mean, or when they were chosen.

Games

Omweso is a traditional board game played with seeds in rows of holes, like the mancala family of games. Show a picture or draw the board. Ludo is also hugely popular. Teach a skipping or hand-clapping game from your own childhood.

Music and dance

Bakisimba is a Baganda dance, and Larakaraka is an Acholi dance. Show a short clip (screened in advance) and teach one easy step. Ask your local community which songs they would suggest.

Arts and crafts

Paper-bead jewellery, basket weaving and bark cloth are well-known Ugandan crafts. Pupils can try paper beads, weave paper strips, or design a pattern in Uganda’s flag colours: black, yellow and red.

School life

Pupils love comparing uniforms, school meals, break time games and how they get to school. Prepare a few photos of a typical day.

Share Uganda fairly. Show modern, varied places as well as villages and wildlife. Pupils may arrive with stereotypes of Africa as one country and one story. Calmly say that Uganda has its own cities, languages, universities and traditions, and let a few good pictures do the work.

Questions pupils often ask: Do you have lions? Is it always hot? Do you eat insects? Do you speak French? What do you eat? Is Uganda dangerous? Prepare a calm, honest, short answer to each, in simple English.

Pupils With Extra Needs

ULIS classes support pupils with learning or developmental differences. These pupils may be accompanied by an AESH, a classroom support worker. Welcome them in the class, invite them to take part, never force them, and ask the teacher or AESH what helps.

Young pupils talk without a filter. A child may say something blunt or unkind. Stay calm. If you feel hurt or threatened, tell the class teacher or your professeur référent and do not carry it alone.

Planning a Session

A 30-minute primary session

  1. Greeting routine and "How do you feel today?" (3 min)
  2. Quick review of last week’s words with flashcards (5 min)
  3. New vocabulary: say, repeat, point, mime (8 min)
  4. Game that uses the words (10 min)
  5. Short song or goodbye routine (4 min)

A 55-minute lycée session

  1. Warm-up question on the board (5 min)
  2. New topic input: picture, clip, or short text (10 min)
  3. Pair work with a speaking goal (15 min)
  4. Whole-class sharing and light correction (15 min)
  5. Quick game or exit question (10 min)

Always: prepare before class, bring a backup activity, do not over-plan, adjust your vocabulary to the age group, and keep pupils busy and involved.

Mini Vocabulary for the Classroom

  • Maternelle — nursery and pre-school years, ages 3 to 6.
  • Primaire — primary school: CP, CE1, CE2, CM1, CM2.
  • Collège / Lycée — lower and upper secondary school.
  • Prof référent — the teacher who looks after you at school.
  • ULIS — school unit for pupils with learning or developmental differences.
  • AESH — classroom assistant who supports pupils with special needs.
  • FLA — foreign language assistant (you).
  • Flashcard / carte image — picture card for a word.

Where to Find Free Materials

  • LearnEnglish Kids (British Council) for songs, stories and games
  • Super Simple for action songs and simple videos
  • Canva for making your own flashcards and posters
  • Your school’s own cupboard, library and the teacher’s textbooks
  • Other assistants, who often share lesson ideas in group chats

AI tools can help you brainstorm, but never rely on them alone. Check facts, keep activities age-appropriate, and always screen any video before you show it.

Remember: you do not have to be a trained teacher. Be prepared, be kind, be curious, and have fun. Expect drawings and hugs from the little ones.
