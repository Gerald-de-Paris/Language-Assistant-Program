Post-Arrival

Life in France

Tasks to complete once you've arrived, and practical info that will help you settle in.

Settling In Checklist

  • At the airport: once you've collected your luggage, use the airport wifi to check your onward transport before heading to the exit
  • Buy a public transport card at an official distribution machine or from a transport official — never buy one from a random person offering to sell you one, this is a common scam
  • Get a French SIM (physical or eSIM) as soon as you can — ask whoever is picking you up, an airport worker, or look for a "Tabac" shop
  • Register with the Embassy of Uganda in France once you're settled — this makes it easier for them to reach or assist you if anything comes up during your stay
  • If your accommodation provides a mailbox, label it with your name so post is actually delivered rather than returned or left unclaimed
  • Carry a little cash and coins, plus a card with money on it, for your first few days
  • For administrative steps, your school secretary and your referent teacher (or the inspector) are your first point of contact; the local tourist office or mairie (town hall) is also useful for practical local information

Visa Validation

You must validate your visa online within your first 3 months in France. If you miss this window, your visa becomes invalid and it becomes illegal for you to stay and work in France. You are also not supposed to travel outside the Schengen Zone before validating your visa.

If you hold a travailleur temporaire visa (most assistants do), your visa validation code is R431-16 8. Double-check this against your own documents, as codes can be updated.
  • Validate your visa here: administration-etrangers-en-france.interieur.gouv.fr
  • If the "timbre électronique" payment link doesn't work, use this alternative: service-public.fr
  • Validation codes reference (updated 2022): interieur.gouv.fr FAQ
  • Issues with validation? Contact dgef-support@interieur.gouv.fr or call 0 806 001 62

Phone & Internet (eSIM)

An eSIM is a digital SIM card that lets you activate a phone plan without a physical SIM. If your phone supports it, you can install a French plan while keeping your original number active on the same device — useful for apps like WhatsApp, banking, or two-factor authentication, while using a French number for calls, texts, and data.

Note: the prices below are rough guides and change over time — check each provider's current pricing before signing up.

Benefits

  • Set it up before leaving, so your phone works immediately on arrival
  • Flexible start dates, so you don't waste paid days while still abroad
  • Keep both your home and French numbers active at once, no need for two phones
  • Everything is managed online — no need to visit a store right away

Free Mobile

  • Generally the most affordable option
  • Popular plan around €9.99/month for unlimited calls/texts in France plus data
  • Sign up and activate online via eSIM
  • No French bank account required — pay by cash or a foreign debit/credit card

Orange

  • More expensive, but reliable coverage, especially outside cities
  • Flexible prepaid and monthly eSIM options
  • Good choice for stronger rural coverage or English-language support
Check that your phone supports eSIMs before ordering (most newer iPhones and Androids do). If you want to keep your home number active, disable roaming data on that line and use your French eSIM for data instead. Consider starting with a month-to-month plan until you know your needs.

Your Journey: Entebbe to Paris, Step by Step

Most routes from Uganda to France involve at least one layover, since there's no direct flight. Here's roughly what the journey looks like end to end.

  • At Entebbe International Airport (EBB): check in and drop your bags, showing your passport, long-stay visa, and your arrêté de nomination (nomination letter) if asked. Confirm your bags are checked all the way through to your final French airport, and keep all physical boarding passes together. You'll then pass through Ugandan immigration departure control and security (laptops out, liquids under 100ml in a clear bag).
  • Your layover — where it is changes what happens next:
    • Transiting through a non-EU hub (e.g. Addis Ababa, Doha, Istanbul, Nairobi): you generally stay inside the international transit area and don't pass immigration again until you land in France.
    • Transiting through an EU/Schengen country (e.g. Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Brussels): this is where you actually enter Europe. You'll show your passport and visa for an entry stamp here, not in France — so keep your documents easily accessible for this stop. Your onward flight into Paris then acts like a domestic hop, with no passport check on arrival.
  • Arriving at Paris Charles de Gaulle (CDG): follow overhead signs for baggage reclaim ("Livraison des bagages / Sortie"), using the free CDGVAL shuttle train if you need to switch terminal buildings. If you didn't already clear passport control at an EU layover, join the "Tous passeports / All Passports" line and present your passport, visa, and nomination letter.
  • Baggage claim & customs: check the arrivals screens for your flight's carousel number, and report to the airline's baggage desk before leaving the secure area if anything is missing or damaged. Most arrivals can walk straight through the green "nothing to declare" channel; just avoid carrying unsealed fresh meat, dairy, or plants, which aren't allowed in.
  • Meeting point & onward transport: once through to the public arrival hall, connect to the free airport Wi-Fi to confirm your pickup or onward transport. Official taxis queue at a clearly marked stand outside (flat rates apply); the RER B train and various public buses also run directly into central Paris — see below for both.

The Airport

Flying into Paris, you'll most likely land at Orly (ORY) or Charles de Gaulle (CDG); the other option is Beauvais (BVA), which is over an hour's bus ride from Paris.

  • Orly: the OrlyVal train (around €12) connects to the RER B; the Orlybus is similarly priced. Metro line 14 also now runs to Orly (around €10), and Orly is included in the Navigo subscription if you've added Zone 5. Tickets for OrlyVal/Orlybus are bought at the airport or Antony station.
  • CDG: included with Navigo, or around €12 for a single ticket via RER B. Leave plenty of time, as delays are common. The RoissyBus is a useful alternative if the RER B is under maintenance. Most international flights currently depart from Terminal 2.
  • Beauvais (BVA): requires a bus (around €17, over an hour). It's suited to budget flights, not practical for arriving with all your luggage for a long stay.
  • On arrival, booking a taxi or rideshare (typically around €50 from CDG or Orly) is often worth it, especially with more than one suitcase.
Safety: only use official, marked taxis or verified rideshare bookings. If using a rideshare app, confirm the license plate matches what's shown, and ask the driver to confirm your name before getting in. Don't accept rides from anyone approaching you at the arrival gates — go to the official taxi stand instead.

Finding the RER B From Each CDG Terminal

  • Terminal 2 (2E, 2F, 2G, 2BD) — where most major carriers land: follow "Train" signs toward the central railway station between Terminals 2C/2D and 2E/2F, then go down to the lower level (Niveau -1) to reach the RER B station. Buy your Navigo or ticket at the automated machines or the Île-de-France Mobilités/RATP counter before the turnstiles.
  • Terminal 3 — mostly budget carriers: a roughly 5-minute covered walkway leads to the Roissypole hub, where the RER B station and ticket machines are right in the main concourse.
  • Terminal 1: take the free automated CDGVAL shuttle to Terminal 3/Roissypole or Terminal 2 to reach the RER B.
  • Public buses are an alternative to the train: RoissyBus (line 352) runs direct to Paris Opéra; bus 350 goes to Gare de l'Est; bus 351 goes to Place de la Nation; and the Noctilien night bus (N140/N143) also serves Gare de l'Est for late arrivals.

Trains

  • Carte Jeune (ages 17–26): bought via the SNCF website, no French bank account required. Discounts on TER trains and the Eurostar to London — typically pays for itself quickly.
  • Carte Adulte (26+): similar to Carte Jeune with slightly smaller discounts, still usually worth it if you travel more than a few times.
  • TGV Max (around €80/month): unlimited TGV trains, but requires a French bank account.
  • Regional discount cards may also exist for your specific area (for example, a Hauts-de-France under-26 card) — worth checking what's available locally, as they can pay for themselves after one use.
  • Watch for occasional seasonal discount promotions on the Oui SNCF site.
  • If you're 27+ and commuting daily by train, check whether your region offers a "weekend pass" add-on to your monthly subscription — in Occitanie, for example, an extra ~€20/month gets unlimited TER weekend travel within the region, which can pay for itself in just one or two trips.
Your school will typically reimburse 50% of your monthly travel costs — keep all paper receipts and ask your secretary for the reimbursement form. Submit it early in the month so it can be processed with your next paycheck. This can usually apply to both a Navigo and a separate regional subscription, not just one.

Strikes (Grèves)

Strikes are common in France. Major strikes are usually announced ahead of time on the SNCF website, though sometimes with only a day's notice.

  • In Paris, Metro lines 1, 4, and 14 usually keep running during strikes, as they're automated
  • Your school will still pay you if you can't make it to work due to a strike — photo proof of cancellations is rarely required, but can't hurt to have
  • Buses often replace metro routes during strikes, but buses can also be affected
  • If travelling, check for planned strikes affecting airport transport (RER B, Air France, etc.)

Paris: The Navigo Pass

The Navigo functions like a rechargeable transit card for the Paris Metro and RER, available as daily, weekly, or monthly passes. The weekly pass runs Monday–Sunday, not 7 days from purchase, so it's best to buy a new monthly pass just before the 1st of the month to avoid queues. A photo is required on your card.

  • Navigo Découverte: available to anyone, obtainable at metro ticket booths
  • Resident Navigo: requires proof of housing in Ile de France; replacing a lost card doesn't require paying the setup fee again
  • Navigo Easy: a plastic top-up card that replaced paper booklets; doesn't require a photo
  • Getting to the airport with a regular ticket instead of an all-zones Navigo can result in a €60 fine — make sure your pass covers the right zones first
If you live in Paris but commute to a school outside the city, ask about the "pass pass" — a €5 card that lets you add a monthly regional subscription (abonnement mensuel) covering your specific commute, often working out far cheaper than paying per trip. Ask at your local station, or a larger nearby one, if yours doesn't offer it.

Using the Metro & RER: A First-Timer's Walkthrough

  • 1. Get your ticket or card. At the automated machines, switch the screen to English. The simplest option is a Navigo Easy card (around €2 for the card itself), which you then load with single rides or packs of tickets, or you can add a Navigo to Apple/Google Wallet on your phone. Warning: if your journey leaves central Paris (for instance, to or from CDG), a regular metro ticket may not be valid — buy a point-to-point ticket or make sure your pass covers the right zones.
  • 2. Pass through the gates. Tap your Navigo card or phone on the purple reader, or insert a paper ticket into the slot, and walk through when the light turns green. Keep your ticket or phone accessible until you're fully outside the station — inspectors (contrôleurs) run frequent random checks, including inside corridors and on platforms, and fines for not having a valid ticket are steep.
  • 3. Find your line and platform. Lines are identified by a number or letter and colour, plus the name of the final station in that direction (the terminus). Pick the direction whose terminus is further along your route, then follow the overhead signs to the right quai (platform). Electronic screens on the platform show how many minutes until the next train.
  • 4. Board and ride. Stand to the side so passengers can get off first. On many older trains the doors don't open automatically — press the green button or lift the silver lever yourself. Keep your phone and bag close to you, especially near the doors, as pickpocketing is more common around busy stops.
  • 5. Transfer or exit. To change lines, follow blue Correspondance signs matching your next line number — you don't need to tap out and back in between metro lines. To leave, follow blue Sortie signs, and check the street map near the platform to pick the exit closest to where you're going.
Before you leave the airport or your accommodation, download an offline map (Google Maps or Citymapper both work) and screenshot the key details of your trip, such as your destination address and any ticket QR codes, so you're not relying on a phone signal underground.

Travel-Day Habits That Save You Trouble

Most of the stress on travel day comes from a handful of avoidable mistakes. A few worth planning around:

  • Luggage limits and tight connections. International weight limits are strict and overweight fees add up quickly. If you have a short layover in Europe, a checked bag can occasionally miss a connection even if you make it. Pack your passport and documents, medication, a change of clothes, and basic toiletries in your carry-on. If your bag doesn't arrive, report it at the airline's baggage desk (Service Bagages) and file a report before leaving the arrivals hall.
  • Tell your bank you're travelling. Foreign debit and credit cards can be blocked at French ticket machines, supermarkets, or train terminals if your bank flags the transaction as unusual. Let your bank know your travel dates beforehand, and carry a small amount of cash in small notes (roughly €100–€150) to cover immediate airport and transport costs until your French bank account is open.
  • Don't depend on a single connection. Airport Wi-Fi and roaming can both fail. Save key addresses, your host's contact, and any train or bus tickets as offline screenshots or PDFs before you leave Uganda, and consider buying a French SIM or setting up an eSIM as soon as you land.
  • Leave generous time at Schengen transfer airports. If your layover is in an EU country such as Amsterdam or Frankfurt, immigration happens there rather than in Paris, and passport queues plus a possible second security check can eat time. Aim for a layover of at least two hours where you can.
  • Build a buffer before onward trains. Flight delays and long passport queues at CDG can cause you to miss a pre-booked train to your region. Avoid booking an onward train for at least 3–4 hours after your scheduled arrival. If you do miss one, show your flight details at the SNCF service desk — staff are often accommodating to international arrivals.
General airport conduct: keep your passport, medication and critical documents in your carry-on rather than checked luggage; never leave your bag unattended or agree to watch a stranger's; don't joke about weapons or threats, which leads to questioning and delays; don't film security or immigration areas, which is prohibited and may result in your phone being confiscated; only use official taxi queues; and check your gate early, since gate changes are common.

Transport Reimbursement

Your school refunds 50% of transport subscriptions for your commute — keep your receipts and fill out the reimbursement form. Individual single-use tickets are not reimbursed, only subscriptions. Reference: service-public.fr.

Navigo reimbursement tip: there's a difference between a tourist Navigo and a personal Navigo. Anything bought on the app or at a ticket counter without providing ID counts as tourist, not personal. If your school secretary accepts printed or emailed tourist receipts, that's fine. If they don't, you'll need a personal Navigo instead, which lets you download a formal attestation with your name and address on it. You can get one either in person at a registration point (bring your ID and proof of address), or online via the IDF Mobilité website under "mon espace" → "mon navigo" → "souscrire un nouveau titre", where you only need to state your address rather than prove it.

Travelling

You must validate your visa and pay the associated fee before travelling outside the EU/Schengen area — see the Visa Validation section above. Some useful planning tools:

  • Multi-modal trip comparison (flights, buses, trains): Omio
  • Flight comparison: Skyscanner or Google Flights — always book directly through the airline once you've found the best option, rather than through a third-party reseller, so you're better protected if there's a cancellation or issue
  • Hostel comparison: Hostelworld — again, book directly with the hostel where possible

Vacances (School Holidays)

Assistants get several holiday periods across the year, typically around October, December, February, and April — but the exact dates vary by academy, so be careful when planning trips with friends posted elsewhere.

Taking time off outside the standard vacances (for emergencies or other reasons) isn't guaranteed to be approved, and schools aren't required to grant it except for genuine emergencies. An unapproved absence can be treated as abandoning your post, risking your contract and visa. If approved, you'll sign a form with your head of establishment confirming the dates, and agree on whether/how you'll make up the hours.

Official vacation calendar: service-public.fr

France splits its regions into three "vacation zones" (Zone A, B, and C) so school holidays don't all fall on the same dates nationwide — which academy you're in determines your zone, and therefore your exact break dates. This is worth knowing when planning trips with assistants from other academies, since your holidays may not line up even in the same general week.

ETIAS & Entry Requirements

ETIAS (a new EU travel authorisation for visa-exempt travellers) has been delayed multiple times. As of mid-2026, it is still not in effect — the EU had targeted late 2026 but recently removed that date from its official pages, with a 2027 launch now considered more likely. The separate Entry/Exit System (EES), which records biometric data at the border, has been live since April 2026.

As a language assistant, you'll already hold a visa, so ETIAS won't apply to you during your contract. It would only become relevant if you wanted to stay in the Schengen Zone after your contract ends (as a visa-exempt tourist), and only if your nationality qualifies for that 90-day visa waiver in the first place. Since this is a fast-moving area, check the official ETIAS site for the current status before relying on any date you read elsewhere, including here.
If you plan to stay in Europe as a tourist after your contract ends, the widely-understood approach (for nationalities eligible for the 90-day visa waiver) is: leave the Schengen Zone before your visa expires, then re-enter at least 24 hours after your visa's expiration date, to get the correct passport stamps starting a fresh 90-day window. Not every nationality qualifies for this waiver — confirm with your embassy. Overstaying is genuinely risky: reported fines for even a day or two over have run into the hundreds of euros, on top of the disruption to any future EU applications — it isn't worth testing.

Housing Assistance (CAF / APL)

This section is unofficial, community-sourced guidance — CAF assistance is never guaranteed. Plenty of assistants go through the whole process without receiving it. Always confirm your own situation with an in-person appointment at your local CAF office.

CAF (Caisse d'Allocations Familiales) can provide housing assistance (APL), but treat it as a bonus, not something to financially depend on — it can take months to arrive, and some assistants never receive it.

  • Apply immediately after signing your lease — CAF payments are retroactive, but only from the date you submit your application, not the start of your lease
  • Your first month of rent is never covered by CAF, no matter when you apply
  • You don't need a Secu number to apply — you can start with just your phone number, and CAF will assign you a temporary identifier
  • Once you do receive your Secu number, create a new CAF account using it — this won't interfere with your existing application under the temporary number
  • Every January, log into your CAF account and file an income declaration to keep receiving payments into the next calendar year
  • When your contract ends (or leaves France), update your address on your CAF account to your home country address to stop payments — if CAF keeps paying you by mistake, they will ask for that money back
  • Renewing assistants should inform CAF of any gap between contract periods

Documents typically needed:

  • Copy of your passport and visa
  • Attestation de domicile
  • Arrêté de nomination
  • Your RIB

Not every apartment qualifies for CAF, and there's no fixed public list of why — your landlord may know, but if they don't, it's worth applying anyway to see if you're accepted. There's also a loose guideline that you live in the housing for 8 months, though enforcement varies by office and by what your lease actually says.

If your landlord wants to receive the CAF payment directly (rather than you), they'll need to fill out a form called attestation de loyer. On this form, "vous" refers to the landlord, not you — if you want the payment yourself, the answer to the question starting "Dans les autres cas..." should be "non." If your landlord receives it instead, you'd typically deduct the CAF amount from what you pay them in rent. Many assistants find it simpler to receive the payment directly themselves and pay full rent to their landlord, but both setups are used.

Official CAF private-market housing guide: caf.fr

Social Security Number (Sécu)

Your Sécu number is used for things like your Carte Vitale (health insurance). The exact process can vary depending on your school level and whether your school has a secretary to help. Commonly, you'll provide your secretary with your passport, visa, birth certificate (in English or French), RIB, and arrête — they submit these on your behalf, and you'll eventually receive a letter with a temporary Sécu number. Your visa generally needs to be validated first.

This process has historically taken up to 6 months for the temporary number, and sometimes over a year for the permanent one and Carte Vitale paperwork, due to backlogs. If you have any pre-existing medical conditions, arrange some form of insurance rather than counting on a fast Sécu.

Ameli (Sécu / Carte Vitale) English-language phone line: +33 9 74 75 36 46

To apply yourself (rather than through your secretary), mail the following to Assurance Maladie de Paris, 75948 Paris Cedex 19:

  • Copy of your birth certificate, passport, and visa (plus your visa validation confirmation)
  • Your RIB
  • Form S1106: ameli.fr
  • A general practitioner (médecin traitant) declaration form: ameli.fr — choose a "secteur 1" doctor near you
  • Your lease and proof of rent payment, and your arrêté de nomination
If your birth certificate name doesn't exactly match your passport (even a small spelling or order difference), this can delay your Sécu application. The usual fix is an Attestation de Concordance from your home country's embassy in France, confirming both documents belong to the same person. The process varies by embassy: research their specific requirements, gather your original documents (especially your birth certificate), contact them to confirm the steps (appointment, mail, or email depending on the embassy), and once received, submit the original attestation to your school secretary to forward to the Sécu system. Some embassies may charge a small fee for this.

Birth Certificate Rules (Worth Getting Right First)

  • The birth certificate you submit should generally have been issued less than a year ago. A short list of countries is exempt from this rule, so check whether yours is on it before ordering a fresh copy.
  • Foreign civil-status documents from many non-European countries must be either legalised or apostilled. The French foreign ministry (diplomatie.gouv.fr) publishes a reference table showing which applies to each country, so look up Uganda there rather than assuming.
  • A multilingual birth extract removes the need for legalisation or apostille. Documents issued by an embassy or consulate are also exempt, but they must state which register they were drawn from (a register number, volume or page reference).
  • A document that was legalised or apostilled less than a year ago is still accepted even if the original is older than a year.
  • If your passport comes from a different country than the one that issued your birth certificate (for example after naturalisation), expect to be asked for a naturalisation certificate too.

What happens after you apply

  • Your complete file goes to the person at your main school who handles this, who checks it and passes it on electronically to the health insurance office
  • You'll first receive a certificate of rights (attestation de droits), and later a "photo" form to complete so that your Carte Vitale can be produced
  • Once you have your permanent number, you can create your personal account on ameli.fr to see reimbursements, download your attestation and forms, and ask questions
  • Ameli's phone line is open Monday to Friday, roughly 8:30 to 17:00. English line: 09 74 75 36 46; in French: 36 46 (say you're in department 75 if you're in Paris)

How your school registers you (worth knowing, so you can follow up)

  • For the official programme, the health insurance office treats your school as your employer and your address as the school's for the duration of your stay. Your school, not you, enters the registration request on a dedicated online portal, so don't try to use that portal yourself
  • If you already have a French social security number, the portal request will be refused and a paper request is needed instead
  • To avoid rejection, the file should have: clear, uncropped scans with no flash glare; your arrêté de nomination (not the installation PV); your birth certificate in its original language even if a translation is also needed; and a fresh file for each new request rather than reusing an old one. Uploads should not overwrite earlier documents
  • Your school can see a status for your file: in progress; waiting number (a temporary number awaiting certification); certified (your number is confirmed and a Carte Vitale can be issued); awaiting documents (they need more from you); closed without follow-up (no reply to a document request within about 2 months); rejected (usually a duplicate or existing number)
  • Once it shows certified, ask your school to tell you, then create your ameli account and download your registration certificate
When you leave France for good: send the health insurance office form S1105 (declaration of transfer of residence outside France), completed and signed, stating your departure date, and return your Carte Vitale. Don't skip this — it closes your file properly.

Pass d'Éducation

As an assistant, you're entitled to a 2-year education pass giving free or discounted entry to museums and cultural sites. Your school secretary can usually help you apply — you'll likely need an ID photo, which you can get from photo booths at metro stations.

Exchanging a Driver's Licence

Exchanging your home-country licence for a French one must be started within your first year of residency (18 months for EU nationals). Some prefectures only count continuous residency under your very first visa; others are more flexible if you've had consecutive visas. Confirm the rule with your specific prefecture, since practice varies.

Only certain countries (and specific US states) are eligible for this exchange — check the official list before starting: official eligibility list (PDF). The process is free if you're eligible.

Documents typically needed:

  • Your official driving record, with a French translation
  • An official French translation of your licence
  • Your original physical licence
  • A scanned colour image of both sides of your licence
  • An identity document (e.g. passport) and proof of legal residence (visa)
  • Proof of residence (e.g. lease)
  • Birth certificate (translated, if not already in an official EU language)
  • A digital photo

Upload everything via the ANTS website. After processing (a few weeks to months), you'll be told where to mail your original licence — use tracked shipping. Your French licence typically arrives by mail a few weeks later.

If you're not eligible, or miss the window, your only route to a French licence is taking the French driving test — a long and expensive process. Also note that exchanging surrenders your original licence: you can't hold both at once, though the exchange can sometimes be reversed later (e.g. exchanging back if you move home).

For a lighter-weight alternative to a full exchange, some assistants look into an International Driving Permit instead — this is a less-documented option, so research current requirements carefully: more info here.

ENIC-NARIC (For Future Master's Applications)

If you're considering applying to a Master's program in France (during or after your assistantship), most programs require an ENIC-NARIC attestation. The process can take 3–6 months, so if there's any chance you'll need it, it's worth starting as early as possible. More info: france-education-international.fr

Attestation sur l'Honneur

This is a signed personal declaration used in France as legal proof for something you're attesting to be true. If you're ever asked for a document you don't have and can't obtain, you can usually provide an attestation sur l'honneur stating so instead. Official simulator/template: service-public.fr

Contract Extension

Most academies don't allow contract extensions. Of those that do, most only allow you to extend up to your visa's expiration date. If your academy is willing to extend you further, you'd also need to extend your visa itself — this means a new contract from the academy with extended dates, plus a visa extension appointment at your local prefecture.

The lists below are historical patterns only, not guarantees — academy policy on extensions changes from year to year and depends on budget. Always contact your own school and rectorat directly, ideally early in the year (around January), rather than relying on this list.

Historically Have Not Allowed Extensions

Versailles, Clermont-Ferrand, Orléans-Tours, Rennes, Toulouse, Créteil, Strasbourg, Grenoble, Bordeaux, Nice

Historically Have Allowed Extensions

Grenoble, Lyon, Amiens, Reims, Lille, Paris, Nantes, Nancy-Metz, Aix-Marseille, Poitiers, Créteil

Note a few academies (like Grenoble and Créteil) appear on both lists — this reflects different assistants' different experiences in different years, underscoring how much this can vary.

If you're hoping for an extension: reach out to your school and the person responsible for assistants at your rectorat directly, as early in the year as possible. You may be asked for a supporting letter from your Prof Référent explaining why the school wants to keep you. Some assistants have found that replying quickly to any extension offer matters, since budget constraints can mean later requests get turned down even when earlier ones for the same academy were approved.
